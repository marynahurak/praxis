Every day we come across statements such as 'a study has shown…', 'the manufacturer recommends…' or 'a colleague advised…'. Yet not all evidence carries equal weight. The classic evidence hierarchy offers a quick way to judge how solid the basis for a given claim is.

## Levels of evidence

**Ia** — meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs).

**Ib** — at least one RCT.

**IIa** — at least one well-designed controlled study without randomisation.

**IIb** — at least one well-designed quasi-experimental study of another type.

**III** — well-designed non-experimental descriptive studies: comparative studies, correlation studies and case-control studies.

**IV** — expert committee reports, or the opinions or clinical experience of respected authorities.

## Grades of recommendation

The AHCPR classification (1992) distinguishes three grades:

**A** (levels Ia, Ib) — recommendations are based on good-quality, consistent studies that include at least one RCT.

**B** (levels IIa, IIb, III) — recommendations are based on well-conducted non-randomised studies.

**C** (level IV) — recommendations are made in the absence of directly applicable studies of good quality.

*Sources: levels of evidence — Shekelle PG, Woolf SH, Eccles M, Grimshaw J. Developing guidelines. BMJ. 1999;318(7183):593. Grades of recommendation A–C — classification of the US Agency for Health Care Policy and Research (AHCPR, 1992).*

## How relevant is this hierarchy today?

The hierarchy is a classic and remains a useful teaching reference, but it should be remembered that it dates from the 1990s. Today the international standard for developing clinical guidelines is the GRADE approach (Guyatt GH et al. BMJ. 2008;336:924–926), while the Oxford CEBM Levels of Evidence (2011) are used for a rapid appraisal of study design.

The fundamental difference is that the classic scheme ranks evidence by study design, whereas GRADE rates the certainty of the entire body of evidence. Certainty may be rated down because of risk of bias, indirectness or imprecision, or rated up when the effect is large. A randomised design therefore does not by itself guarantee high certainty if the trial was poorly conducted.

## Why this matters particularly in prosthetics and orthotics

Our field relies predominantly on the lower levels of the evidence pyramid. This is not a sign of negligence but an objective reality. Randomising a prosthetic intervention and blinding participants is almost impossible; samples are small and heterogeneous; and withholding a device from a patient for the sake of a control group is unethical. Most of the evidence in our field therefore comes from cohort and comparative studies, case series and expert consensus.

The conclusion is not that 'our science is poor' but the opposite: the ability to critically appraise lower-level evidence is a core professional skill for prosthetists-orthotists. This is precisely why lower-level studies, including non-experimental descriptive studies (level III), have real value when they are appraised correctly.

## Checklist: appraising any source with five questions

1. **What is the level of evidence?** A meta-analysis, an RCT, a cohort study, a case series or expert opinion?
2. **Who funded the study?** In our field, a substantial proportion of research is funded by manufacturers. A conflict of interest does not make the data wrong, but it does call for more careful appraisal.
3. **What are the sample and the population?** Sample size and amputation level: are the participants similar to your patient?
4. **Are the outcome measures valid?** Were validated outcome measures used (PLUS-M, PEQ, OPUS, TAPES), or is the conclusion based on a subjective impression?
5. **Is this the primary source or a secondary publication?** Have you read the original article rather than an account of it?
