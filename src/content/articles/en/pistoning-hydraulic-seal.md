The patient insists the prosthesis “fits tight”. Under static load everything looks flawless. But as soon as the foot leaves the ground and swing phase begins, the socket lags behind the residual limb — and every subsequent heel strike becomes a micro-impact on tissue that has already survived a blast injury.

Pistoning is usually framed as a sizing problem: “the socket is too big — make it smaller.” I propose looking at it as an engineer would: as a seal that can no longer hold a pressure differential.

## Methodology and mechanics

In swing phase the prosthesis is subjected to a distraction force: the mass of the components plus inertial forces that grow with walking speed. Three mechanisms resist it:

1. **Friction** at the liner–socket interface.
2. **Mechanical locking** — pin lock, strap, lanyard.
3. **Pressure differential** in the sealed volume between the liner and the socket wall.

The third mechanism behaves like a hydraulic seal. As long as the sleeve or seal-in ring holds, any attempt to withdraw the limb creates negative pressure that pulls the socket along. Once an air channel appears — a fold in the liner, a worn sleeve, a change in limb volume over the day — the pressure equalises. Suspension does not fail all at once: it bleeds off with every step.

Hence the clinical paradox: a limb can be tightly contained under static load and still not be held in dynamics. Fit and suspension are different quantities, and they need different tests.

## Critical audit

- Methods of assessing pistoning vary: radiography, photogrammetry, motion capture, embedded sensors. Absolute values obtained with different methods are not directly comparable.
- Static loading in the fitting room does not reproduce the inertial forces of real walking, so it may underestimate displacement.

## Clinical takeaway for the MDT

1. **Prosthetist.** Before replacing the suspension system, check the seal: the condition of the sleeve, liner folds, air channels at the socket interface.
2. **Whole team.** Assess daily fluctuations in residual limb volume: morning and evening pistoning may have different causes and different solutions.
3. **Physical therapist.** A shortened step or cautious weight transfer may be compensation for unstable suspension, not just a deficit in strength or balance.
