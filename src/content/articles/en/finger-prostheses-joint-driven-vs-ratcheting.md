A comparison of disability measured with the DASH found a mean score of 49 in people with partial hand amputation and 39 in people with major unilateral upper limb amputation ([Davidson, 2004, as cited in Stevens](https://opedge.com/updates-on-prosthetic-rehabilitation-following-partial-hand-amputation/)). Higher scores indicate greater limitation, so the 'smaller' amputation was associated with greater disability.

Dozens of devices are available for this patient group, yet I found no study that directly compares two widely used mechanical systems, Naked Prosthetics and Point Designs. The question 'which is better?' therefore has no evidence-based answer yet. There is, however, an answer to a different question: which control principle suits a particular hand.

## Two control principles

**Joint-driven digit (Naked Prosthetics: MCPDriver, PIPDriver, ThumbDriver).** The prosthetic finger is linked to the residual phalanx by a linkage mechanism: the patient flexes their own joint, and the prosthesis flexes in synchrony with the other fingers.

It works rather like a bicycle drivetrain: every movement and every newton of force is generated by the user. Because the position of the prosthesis is coupled to the position of the user's own joint, the patient 'feels' the finger through that joint. This is the principle of extended physiological proprioception (EPP), described by Simpson (1974) for body-powered prostheses. *Mechanistic reasoning.*

**Passive ratcheting digit (Point Designs: Point Digit, Point Partial; also GripLock by Naked Prosthetics).** The finger is not driven by a joint: the patient positions it by pressing it against an object or surface, and a ratchet mechanism locks it in place. The finger is released by pressing a button or by full flexion, after which a spring returns it to extension ([Segil et al., 2021](https://scholar.colorado.edu/downloads/w0892c466)).

It resembles the ratchet of a winch: the mechanism holds a load that the muscles could not sustain, but it does not generate movement itself.

### The mechanics that determine the choice

*Mechanistic reasoning (level 6 of the Praxis hierarchy).*

- **The fingertip force of a driver is limited by the moment generated at the user's own MCP joint.** Fingertip force equals this moment divided by the length of the lever arm (F = τ / r): the longer the restored finger, the lower the fingertip force.
- **The same moment is transmitted through the ring to the residual phalanx.** The shorter the phalanx, the shorter the lever arm and the higher the pressure on the skin. For scars after blast injury and for skin grafts, this is often the decisive factor.
- **A driver has no locking function.** Holding an object requires sustained muscle contraction; [Miller (2023)](https://oaepublish.com/articles/2347-9264.2022.140) notes that body-powered prostheses can cause considerable fatigue.
- **A ratcheting digit does not depend on muscle strength.** The load limit is determined by the design and the interface rather than by the patient's muscles. The trade-off is a set of discrete positions ([11 in the Point Digit](https://scholar.colorado.edu/downloads/w0892c466), [7 in the Point Partial](https://WWW.OPCHEALTH.COM.AU/images/uploaded/PDFs/Point%20Partial%20Product%20Overview-US.pdf)) and an additional pre-positioning step before each grasp.

### Indication map (according to manufacturer data)

| Amputation level | Active residual joint | Options |
|---|---|---|
| Middle or distal phalanx (II–V), functional PIP | Yes | PIPDriver |
| Proximal phalanx (II–V), functional MCP, sufficient length for the ring | Yes | MCPDriver or Point Partial (amputation near the PIP) — **this is where the real choice arises** |
| At the MCP level or more proximal (II–V) | No | Point Digit / Digit Mini, GripLock |
| Thumb, proximal phalanx, functional MCP | Yes | ThumbDriver |

Sources for indications: [Naked Prosthetics brochure](https://media.ossur.com/ossur-dam/image/upload/v1680663904/product-documents-global/NP_Family_Brochure_en_au_23.pdf), [Point Partial overview](https://WWW.OPCHEALTH.COM.AU/images/uploaded/PDFs/Point%20Partial%20Product%20Overview-US.pdf), [Segil et al., 2021](https://scholar.colorado.edu/downloads/w0892c466).

Tellingly, Naked Prosthetics also makes the passive GripLock: even the manufacturer of joint-driven digits acknowledges that such devices do not suit every patient. It is the control principles, not the brands, that should be compared. It is also worth noting that both brands now belong to large companies: [Naked Prosthetics to Össur (since 2022)](https://opedge.com/ossur-acquires-naked-prosthetics/) and [Point Designs to Hanger (2025 agreement)](https://opedge.com/hanger-acquiring-point-designs/).

## What the research shows: a critical appraisal

**Point Designs**

- **The Point Digit II has undergone bench testing only** ([Segil et al., 2021](https://scholar.colorado.edu/downloads/w0892c466)):
  - static fingertip load to failure of approximately 1370 N;
  - 263,581 ± 11,837 flexion cycles; the authors equate this to roughly 3 years of use, but only under an assumed usage pattern (30 grasps per hour over an 8-hour working day);
  - the study did not involve human participants; all authors are employees or major shareholders of Point Designs.
- **Rated load according to manufacturer materials:** 150 lb, i.e. approximately 68 kg ([Point Partial overview](https://WWW.OPCHEALTH.COM.AU/images/uploaded/PDFs/Point%20Partial%20Product%20Overview-US.pdf)).
- **Clinical take-home study** ([NCT04755790](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04755790)): 13 participants, status completed. I found no published results.
- **The Point Partial study** ([NCT05012683](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05012683)) is a single-arm study with 11 participants: outcomes are compared only with the baseline without a prosthesis, and alternative systems are not investigated.

**Naked Prosthetics**

- **Young et al. (2019), n = 1:** a 72-year-old patient with an index finger amputation at the PIP level; the MCPDriver was compared with a 3D-printed prosthesis ([Young et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41205-019-0044-0)).
  - Box and Block Test: 18 blocks per minute without a prosthesis, 22 with each of the two prostheses and 30 with the unaffected hand.
  - Device satisfaction on the QUEST: MCPDriver 2.5 ± 0.5; 3D-printed prosthesis (material cost approximately USD 20) 3.3 ± 1.2.
- **Graham et al. (2022), n = 1:** amputation of digits II–V at the level of the proximal phalanges, fitted with MCPDrivers ([Graham et al., 2022](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/b3ffd76fa1d7a6c9853f15c84f4cb205/PMC9007195.pdf)).
  - DASH 8.3; the patient returned to office work.
  - With the prosthesis, the patient lifted loads of up to approximately 4.5 kg (10 lb).
  - The interface had to be redesigned three times, and the silicone rings had to be downsized within a week because of volume loss in the residual phalanges.
  - The authors explicitly name the Point Partial and GripLock as possible options should heavier lifting be required.
- **I found no published data on the load capacity or service life of the drivers.** For the GripLock, the manufacturer states a load capacity of 90 kg and at least 300,000 cycles ([NP brochure](https://media.ossur.com/ossur-dam/image/upload/v1680663904/product-documents-global/NP_Family_Brochure_en_au_23.pdf)).

**A contradiction worth noting.** The developers of the Point Digit emphasise that the ratchet mechanism allows the finger to be positioned with one hand ([Segil et al., 2021](https://scholar.colorado.edu/downloads/w0892c466)). The independent review by [Miller (2023)](https://oaepublish.com/articles/2347-9264.2022.140) notes that ratcheting digits can be difficult to use because positioning requires the other hand. Both statements may hold true depending on the task. For a patient with polytrauma whose other hand is also injured, however, this difference is fundamental.

**Population context**

- **Silicone prosthesis** (n = 42): hand function on the SHAP barely changed. Tip pinch strength decreased from 40.4 to 34.4 (p = 0.029), whereas power grip strength did not change ([Kuret et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0309364615596064)).
- **Employment:** fewer than half of people with partial hand amputation returned to the same job; fewer than a third regularly wore a silicone prosthesis at work ([Burger et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/09638280701320763)).
- **Prosthetic provision** (n = 60): among those who attended hand therapy, only 37.1% received a prosthesis. The mean DASH score was 50.3, and 25% of patients subsequently developed finger stiffness ([Allen et al., PSRC 2026 abstract](https://zenodo.org/records/20088337)).
- **Psychological burden** (n = 305): compared with more proximal levels of limb loss, partial hand amputation was associated with higher odds of pain interfering with daily life and of a positive PTSD screen ([Kearns et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29553789/)). For patients with combat injuries, this is a clinical priority in its own right.
- **Assessment tools:** there are still no instruments developed specifically for partial hand amputation. Clinicians most often use the DASH, the Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT), the Box and Block Test and range-of-motion measurement ([Carlyle et al., 2024](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/89580/)).

## Implications for the MDT: my clinical position

Until head-to-head studies are available, I choose a system on the basis of the biomechanics of the individual hand, not the brand.

**1. Assessment before choosing a system:**

- active range of motion at the residual MCP or PIP joint, measured with a goniometer;
- length of the residual phalanx;
- skin condition: scars, skin flaps, hypersensitivity, neuromas;
- condition of the other hand;
- the patient's main tasks: load-bearing work or fine manipulation;
- number of affected digits.

**2. When I lean towards a driver:** the joint is preserved and functional, the phalanx is long enough for the ring, the skin tolerates loading, and the patient needs fast bimanual activities (keyboard, kitchenware, small objects).

**3. When I lean towards a ratcheting digit:**

- a fixed contracture or pain on joint movement;
- a phalanx that is too short, or vulnerable skin;
- heavy manual work, hook grip, carrying loads.

**4. Combinations.** Different systems can be combined on different digits of the same hand, for example a driver on the index finger and a ratcheting digit on the middle finger.

**5. For surgeons.** If prosthetic fitting is a long-term goal, preserve the length of the proximal phalanx: for a driver, every millimetre counts both as lever arm and as contact area ([Graham et al., 2022](https://rcastoragev2.blob.core.windows.net/b3ffd76fa1d7a6c9853f15c84f4cb205/PMC9007195.pdf)).

**6. For physical therapists:**

- restore range of motion and carry out desensitisation before the prosthesis is fabricated, not afterwards;
- for ratcheting digits, train pre-positioning strategies;
- for all patients, prevent overuse of the unaffected hand.

**7. Measure before and after.** A minimum set: QuickDASH, the Box and Block Test, the Patient-Specific Functional Scale (PSFS), wear time and photographic documentation of skin condition. Where possible, provide the patient with a trial device before the final prescription: roughly half of the clinicians surveyed do so and report only positive experiences ([Carlyle et al., 2024](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/89580/)).

1. Who among you has fitted both systems? What ultimately proved decisive: range of motion, skin condition or the patient's occupation?

## Dilemma for the community

**Dilemma (simulated teaching case).** A 31-year-old man with a blast injury. Amputation of digits II–III at the level of the proximal phalanges; active MCP flexion of 0–55° with pain at end range; a hypersensitive scar. He works in construction. What would you choose?

- MCPDriver: synchronous flexion driven by his own MCP joints.
- Passive ratcheting digits: load-bearing independent of MCP range of motion.
- Combination: a driver on one finger, a ratcheting digit on the other.
- First 6–8 weeks of therapy (ROM, desensitisation), then decide.
