A patient who comes to an osseointegration consultation rarely starts from zero. Behind them there are usually years of struggling with a socket: dermatitis, fluctuating residual limb volume, pain at the ischial seat, endless refits. This is why before-and-after studies are almost bound to show an effect: their baseline is the worst period of the person's prosthetic history. Comparing bone-anchored fixation with a socket that has already failed is like testing a new hydraulic damper against one that is already leaking — the win is guaranteed, but it says nothing about how the new part compares with one that works.

[A systematic review published in July 2026 in *Orthopaedic Surgery*](https://doi.org/10.1111/os.70369) asks a less comfortable question: how do users of bone-anchored prostheses compare with people for whom a socket works? The answer is no statistically significant difference in mobility, gait speed or quality of life. I have analysed the full text, and I consider this null result one of the most useful findings of the year — not because it settles the question, but because it shows precisely which question we still cannot answer.

## Methods and mechanics

### Review design

The British authors deliberately excluded single-arm studies, in which the same patients serve as their own controls before and after surgery. Eligibility was restricted to two-arm comparative studies: a separate group using a bone-anchored prosthesis (BAP) compared with a separate group using a socket-suspended prosthesis (SSP).

- **Protocol and reporting:** registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251137214), reported according to PRISMA.
- **Search:** five databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, Cochrane, Web of Science), English-language publications only; last search on 13 December 2024.
- **Quality assessment:** risk of bias with ROBINS-I V2, certainty of evidence with GRADE.
- **Synthesis:** random-effects meta-analysis only where at least three studies reported the same outcome; the remaining results were synthesised narratively.

Twelve studies published between 2005 and 2024 met the criteria. Although the protocol also allowed cohort designs, all twelve turned out to be cross-sectional: both groups were assessed once, with no follow-up over time.

- **Countries:** five studies were conducted in Sweden, four in Germany, and the rest in the Netherlands, Italy, the USA and the UK; two of them were international.
- **Implants:** the screw-type OPRA system in five studies; press-fit systems of various generations in the rest (TOPS/EEP in three, ILP and OIP in one each); in two studies the implant type was not stated.
- **Participants:** mostly after traumatic amputation. Mean age across studies was 43–62 years, mean time since amputation ranged from about 7 to 29 years, and time with a bone-anchored prosthesis was 2–16 years.

In other words, these are experienced civilian users with many years of prosthetic use behind them.

### What direct skeletal attachment changes biomechanically

*Mechanistic reasoning (level 6 of the Praxis hierarchy) unless stated otherwise.*

The meta-analysis found no difference in global measures such as TUG and gait speed. Individual studies, however, report changes exactly where first principles would predict them — in the region directly affected by the proximal part of the socket.

**Hip range of motion.** The proximal trim lines of a socket — the ischial seat, the medial wall and the anterior brim — mechanically limit hip flexion, extension and abduction. [The systematic review by Rehani et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fresc.2024.1336042) likewise notes that a socket can restrict hip movement and make sitting difficult.

The authors of the 2026 review explain the greater range of motion differently, through differences in muscle activation: socket users continuously engage their muscles for stability, whereas bone-anchored prosthesis users use them solely for walking. The two hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, but they are worth distinguishing because they point to different interventions. The first calls for reconsidering socket design; the second, for work on neuromuscular control.

**Step width.** A wider base of support is a classic compensation in transfemoral prosthesis users: the femur shifts within the soft tissues inside the socket, and the medial wall presses into the perineal region. With direct skeletal attachment the femur is rigidly coupled to the prosthesis, so the need for this compensation may decrease.

Here I differ from the authors in emphasis. They interpret a narrower step as something that may enhance stability. Biomechanically, in my view, it is more accurate to say that a narrower step indicates less need to widen the base of support, rather than being an independent source of stability.

**Osseoperception.** Soft tissue and the liner act as a low-pass filter: they damp high-frequency vibration before it reaches the bone. A titanium fixture attached directly to bone transmits vibration with less attenuation. This is consistent with the finding of Häggström et al.: bone-anchored prosthesis users detected vibration at 125 and 250 Hz better, whereas there was no difference at lower frequencies. Where exactly this sensation originates, and whether it improves balance, has not been established; the review authors also consider the clinical significance of this result unclear.

## Key findings

**Meta-analyses** ([Tan Sue Wei et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/os.70369)):

| Outcome | Studies | Participants (BAP : SSP) | Mean difference (95% CI) | GRADE certainty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Timed Up and Go (TUG), s | 3 | 41 : 38 | 0.16 (−2.09 to 2.41) | very low |
| Activities-specific Balance Confidence (ABC) | 3 | 29 : 29 | 0.05 (−22.19 to 22.29) | very low |
| Q-TFA: global score | 3 | 52 : 44 | 1.14 (−22.02 to 24.30) | very low |
| Q-TFA: prosthetic mobility | 3 | 52 : 44 | 0.37 (−9.93 to 10.68) | low |
| Q-TFA: problems | 3 | 52 : 44 | −6.29 (−18.77 to 6.19) | very low |
| Q-TFA: prosthesis use | 4 | 60 : 52 | 1.18 (−2.36 to 4.71) | very low |
| Gait speed, m/s | 3 | 39 : 38 | −0.03 (−0.31 to 0.25) | very low |

None of the pooled outcomes reached statistical significance. Heterogeneity was high for the ABC scale (I² = 76.3%). Q-TFA is the Questionnaire for Persons with a Transfemoral Amputation.

**Outside the meta-analysis** (one or two studies per outcome):

- **Step width:** narrower in the bone-anchored group in two studies (Welke et al., 2023; Ranaldi et al., 2023; p = 0.001), with no difference in a third (Kooiman et al., 2024).
- **Hip range of motion:**
  - Hagberg et al. (2005): flexion–extension 127° vs 97°, rotation significantly greater (p < 0.001);
  - Welke et al. (2023): abduction–adduction 23.2° vs 18.0° (p = 0.03), flexion–extension 48.2° vs 44.3° (p = 0.064).

  The absolute values in the two studies differ by a factor of about 2.6 (127° vs 48°), which points to different measurement protocols. They cannot be pooled, and the review authors do not attempt to do so.
- **Osseoperception:**
  - Häggström et al. (2014): better detection of vibration at 125 and 250 Hz (p = 0.01 and 0.03);
  - Örgel et al. (2023): better discrimination of material hardness — 7.1 ± 1.1 vs 5.4 ± 1.2 points (p < 0.001).
- **Costs** (one Swedish study, Häggström et al., 2013):
  - annual prosthetic costs 14% lower with bone-anchored fixation (€3,149 vs €3,672), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.632);
  - manufacturing the prosthesis itself cost more (€9,370 vs €4,890);
  - prosthetic service visits: 3.1 vs 7.2 per year (p < 0.0001).
- **Safety:** no two-arm study of infectious complications was identified.

## Critical appraisal

### 1. Who exactly was compared

The main limitation is not sample size but group composition. Candidates for bone-anchored fixation are carefully selected. Among the criteria listed by the authors themselves:

- problems with the socket;
- adequate bone quality and residual limb length;
- no smoking, diabetes, vascular disease, immunosuppression or active infection.

The comparison group consists of people who use a socket successfully; the authors describe them as functioning at a high level and content with their prostheses. What is being compared, then, is not two methods for the same patient but two different populations. This is classic confounding by indication.

As a result, the null finding supports two opposite readings:

- **pessimistic:** bone-anchored fixation offers no advantage over a well-fitted socket;
- **optimistic:** people for whom a socket did not work reached, after osseointegration, the level of those for whom it does.

A cross-sectional design cannot distinguish between these scenarios. Both, however, lead to the same clinical conclusion, which is consistent with [the review by Rehani et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fresc.2024.1336042). According to that review, bone-anchored implants are cost-effective for people who face significant challenges in using a socket, but may offer no additional advantage to those who are functioning well with one.

### 2. Survivorship bias

*This is my own methodological observation; it is not raised in the review.* A cross-sectional study captures only those who are using a prosthesis at the time of assessment:

- people whose implant was removed because of infection or fracture do not enter the bone-anchored group;
- those who abandoned prosthesis use altogether enter neither group.

With 2–16 years of bone-anchored prosthesis use, the BAP group is probably enriched with successful cases. These data therefore describe outcomes for those in whom the method worked, not for everyone to whom it was offered.

### 3. "No difference" does not mean "the same"

The confidence intervals are too wide to support equivalence:

- for the ABC scale (0–100 points), the interval spans roughly ±22 points;
- for the Q-TFA global score, from −22 to +24 points;
- for gait speed, from −0.31 to +0.25 m/s.

Such bounds are compatible with a noticeable advantage and with a noticeable disadvantage for either method. The individual meta-analyses included between 58 and 112 participants. Here, absence of evidence of an effect is not evidence of absence of an effect — which is exactly why GRADE rates certainty as very low for six of the seven outcomes.

### 4. A scale built for the world of sockets

The Q-TFA was developed for socket users. It centres on the usual socket complaints and contains nothing on infection after surgery, a gap the authors acknowledge. The instrument sees only the problems that exist in the world of sockets, so some of the risks of bone-anchored fixation simply fall outside its view.

The PMQ 2.0 tells a similar story: a significant advantage for bone-anchored fixation on this questionnaire came only from two retrospective studies by the same author group. The review authors themselves point to possible selection bias.

### 5. Safety outside the frame

There is no comparative study of infection. The scale of the issue can be seen from single-arm data: in [the meta-analysis by Milchem et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12306-026-00946-9) (22 studies, 979 patients), the pooled rate of infectious complications was 36% and revision-free survival at 10 years was 77%. Functional parity with the socket has to be weighed against this risk profile, not considered in isolation.

### 6. Heterogeneity and unmeasured factors

- Screw-type and press-fit implants were pooled without subgroup analysis.
- Reporting of prosthetic knee type and functional level was inconsistent, as the authors acknowledge. These are precisely the factors that strongly influence TUG and gait speed.
- Rehabilitation protocols differed between studies.

### 7. Internal discrepancies worth noticing

They do not change the conclusions, but they show why the tables deserve as much attention as the abstract:

- The abstract states 200 and 244 participants, whereas the demographics table adds up to 213 and 248.
- Two studies from the same group (Kooiman et al., 2023 and 2024) have identical demographic characteristics in the table — probably the same 20 people. The authors warn in general terms about possible cohort overlap, so the number of unique participants is lower than the total.
- For Pospiech et al. (2021), the group sizes are given in reverse order in two tables (22/17 and 17/22).
- The abstract and the opening of the discussion mention "lower long-term costs" without noting that in the only cost study the difference was not statistically significant.

### 8. Registration and search currency

The PROSPERO number carries a 2025 prefix, whereas the last search is dated 13 December 2024. The protocol was therefore most likely registered after the search. More than eighteen months passed between the search and publication (2 July 2026), so studies from 2025–2026 were not included.

### Strengths

These deserve to be named as well:

- independent funding (NIHR Barts Biomedical Research Centre) and a declaration of no conflicts of interest;
- open access (CC BY 4.0), with data in the supplementary materials;
- a GRADE assessment and candidly stated limitations.

The underlying idea — comparing independent groups rather than before-and-after states — is sound and much needed.

## Implications for the MDT

**For prosthetists and orthotists:**

- **Position.** The available data support the following working position: osseointegration is a solution for people for whom a socket does not work, not an "upgrade" for a successful socket user. Before referral, it is worth documenting that socket options have genuinely been exhausted; I proposed a minimum documentation set in [my analysis of the state pilot project](../state-funded-osseointegration-ukraine/).
- **Patient expectations.** During counselling, it helps to separate expectations clearly. These data do not promise better TUG or gait speed than with a well-fitted socket. The potential gains lie elsewhere: hip range of motion, sitting, osseoperception, and freedom from socket–skin interface problems. They are weighed against a permanent stoma and the risk of infection.
- **Workload.** Fewer service visits (3.1 vs 7.2 per year in one Swedish study) change the nature of the work: fewer socket refits, more attention to component set-up.

**For physical therapists:**

- **What to measure.** Assessing bone-anchored prosthesis users only with TUG and gait speed may miss exactly what the method changes. Add hip goniometry and step width during gait analysis.
- **The stoma.** Supplement the Q-TFA with structured documentation of the stoma and of infection episodes: the scale does not capture infection after surgery.
- **Essential variables.** Always record functional level and knee type: their absence from the primary studies became one of the limitations of this review.

**For the health system:**

- **A different population.** Participants in these studies were civilian users many years after amputation. In Ukraine, a substantial share of candidates are service members and veterans with combat injuries, who undergo surgery much earlier. In [the first Ukrainian OPRA cohort](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00264-026-07054-0), the median time from amputation to surgery was seven months ([according to the manufacturer's press release](https://www.placera.se/pressmeddelanden/integrum-new-clinical-study-shows-high-implant-survival-with-the-opra-implant-system-in-war-injured-veterans-in-ukraine-20261014)). The review's findings can be extrapolated to them only with great caution.
- **An opportunity for the pilot project.** The state pilot project could provide what the international literature lacks — prospective comparative data. That would require:
  - a parallel group of patients with the same indications who remain on a socket;
  - identical assessment instruments in both groups;
  - standardised recording of infections;
  - follow-up beyond 12 months.

## Questions for the community

1. If osseointegration "only" brings a person for whom a socket failed up to the level of a successful socket user, is that a disappointment — or exactly the result we should honestly prepare patients for?
2. Who should be the comparison group in a Ukrainian osseointegration registry: patients with the same indications who remain on a socket, or all users of transfemoral prostheses?

## Dilemma for the community

**Dilemma (simulated teaching case).** A 34-year-old man with a transfemoral amputation three years ago. He uses a socket with vacuum suspension, has no skin problems and walks without walking aids. He asks for osseointegration "to walk better". What is your next step?

- Explain that an advantage over a working socket is unproven, and do not refer.
- Find out what he wants to improve: sitting, hip movement, feeling the prosthesis.
- Refer to the OI team for a consultation with a detailed discussion of risks.
- Optimise the current prosthesis and revisit the question in 6 months.
